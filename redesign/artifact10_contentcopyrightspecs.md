# MAKATA.ai — Content & Copywriting Specification

**Artifact 10 — Positioning, Messaging, Voice, Terminology & Page Copy System**

---

# 1. Purpose

This artifact defines how MAKATA.ai communicates.

The goal is not to produce polished marketing language for its own sake.

The goal is to ensure that every page communicates the same underlying identity:

> **MAKATA.ai is a working laboratory for building, testing, and understanding AI systems.**

The site should communicate technical capability through:

- actual systems
- experiments
- demonstrations
- constraints
- measurements
- failures
- observations
- decisions
- unresolved questions

rather than through exaggerated claims.

---

# 2. Core Positioning

## Primary positioning

> **AI systems, Edge AI, and experimental engineering.**

Expanded positioning:

> **MAKATA.ai is an engineering laboratory where AI systems are built, tested, and explored under real-world constraints.**

This should be the conceptual center of the website.

---

# 3. What MAKATA.ai Is

MAKATA.ai is:

- an AI engineering laboratory
- a portfolio of working systems
- an experimentation environment
- a technical knowledge base
- a demonstration surface
- a record of engineering decisions

It is simultaneously:

```text
BUILD
  ↓
TEST
  ↓
MEASURE
  ↓
UNDERSTAND
  ↓
CHANGE
  ↓
TEST AGAIN
```

---

# 4. What MAKATA.ai Is Not

Avoid positioning the site as:

- a generic AI consultancy
- a software agency
- a startup incubator
- a personal résumé
- an AI news site
- an academic research publication
- a collection of polished product landing pages
- a generic “AI thought leader” website

Some of those activities may exist in the user's professional work.

They should not define MAKATA.ai.

---

# 5. Core Messaging Idea

The central message should be:

> **Don't take my word for what I can build. Look at the systems.**

That idea should influence the entire site.

Instead of:

> “I am an expert in Edge AI.”

Prefer:

> “I build AI systems that run under real device constraints.”

Then show the system.

---

# 11. Voice Test

Before publishing a sentence, ask:

> **Would an experienced engineer believe this sentence?**

If not, rewrite it.

Second test:

> **Can I point to evidence on the site supporting this claim?**

If not, weaken the claim or remove it.

---

# 12. Confidence Language

MAKATA.ai should distinguish between:

### Measured

> “Latency measured at 1.8 seconds across 20 runs.”

### Observed

> “The application began restarting more frequently during sustained inference.”

### Inferred

> “The behavior appears consistent with thermal or memory pressure.”

### Hypothesis

> “Thermal throttling may be contributing to the latency increase.”

Never collapse these into:

> “Thermal throttling caused the latency increase.”

unless it was actually established.

---

# 13. Epistemic Labels

Use visible labels when useful:

```text

OBSERVED
INFERRED
HYPOTHESIS
```

These labels are particularly useful for:

- experiments
- field notes
- benchmarks
- technical explanations

They should not be plastered onto ordinary marketing copy.

# 15. “AI-Powered” Rule

Avoid:

> AI-powered document intelligence platform.

Prefer:

> An on-device document intelligence system using local AI models.

The second tells the visitor something testable.

---

# 16. Technical Depth

MAKATA.ai should not hide technical details in order to appear approachable.

Instead use progressive disclosure.

Example:

```text
Simple
↓
Technical
↓
Deep technical
```

A recruiter can understand the first level.

An engineering leader can inspect the second.

A technical peer can go deeper.

---

# 25. Project Status Language

Use explicit statuses:

```text

```

---

# 26. Failure Language

Failures should be written neutrally.

Instead of:

> “Unfortunately, Gemma wasn't good enough.”

Prefer:

> **What didn't work**

> Consecutive document-chat interactions produced increasing latency and device heat. The behavior made the approach unsuitable for the intended interaction pattern under the tested conditions.

Then document what changed.

---

# 27. Constraint Language

Constraints should be framed as engineering variables.

Instead of:

> “The iPhone has limited resources.”

Prefer:

> **Constraint — Memory**

> Available device memory limited the practical model size and increased the probability of application restarts under sustained inference.

Only state this if supported by actual observation or measurement.

---

# 28. Benchmark Language

Never present an isolated benchmark number.

Bad:

> **1.4 sec latency**

Better:

> **1.4 sec median latency**

Then:

```text
Model
Device
Input size
Number of runs
Runtime
Date
Method
```

---

# 29. Comparison Language

Avoid:

> “Model A is better than Model B.”

Prefer:

> “Model A produced lower latency under the tested conditions, while Model B produced higher output quality.”

The visitor can then understand the trade-off.

# 35. “What Changed?” Pattern

This should become one of MAKATA.ai's recurring editorial devices.

Structure:

```text
BEFORE

Initial approach

↓

OBSERVATION

What happened

↓

CHANGE

What was modified

↓

AFTER

What changed in behavior
```

This makes engineering evolution visible.

---

# 36. “What Didn't Work?” Pattern

Another recurring structure:

```text
APPROACH
What was attempted?

EXPECTED
What was supposed to happen?

OBSERVED
What actually happened?

DECISION
What changed?

LESSON
What was learned?
```

This prevents retrospective storytelling from becoming artificially clean.

---

# 37. Anti-Coherence Rule

MAKATA.ai must not turn every project into a neat success story.

A project may contain:

```text
Successful result
+
Unexpected result
+
Contradictory evidence
+
Failed approach
+
Unresolved question
```

All five are valid content.

Do not remove contradictions merely to make the narrative cleaner.

---

# 38. Anti-Hype Rule

If a claim sounds impressive but cannot be demonstrated, measured, or explained:

> **Downgrade the language.**

Example:

Instead of:

> “A breakthrough in local AI.”

Use:

> “An experiment in running document intelligence locally.”

Evidence can later justify stronger language.

# 39. Personal Positioning

Avoid:

> “I am a visionary AI leader with extensive expertise in cutting-edge technologies.”

Prefer:

> “I build AI systems, evaluate their behavior under constraints, and investigate what changes when the assumptions behind them stop holding.”

The work then demonstrates the claim.

---

# 48. Terminology Consistency

Choose one term and maintain it.

For example:

```text
on-device AI
```

should generally be preferred over randomly alternating:

```text
on-device AI
local AI
local-first AI
offline AI
edge AI
device AI
```

These terms can all be valid, but they should have distinct meanings.

---

# 49. Terminology Definitions

Recommended distinction:

### On-device AI

Inference happens directly on the user's device.

### Local-first

The product architecture prioritizes local processing, potentially with controlled exceptions.

### Offline

The system can function without an internet connection.

### Edge AI

AI computation occurs outside centralized cloud infrastructure, potentially on devices or edge infrastructure.

These should not be treated as interchangeable marketing terms.

---

# 50. Privacy Claims

Privacy claims must describe architecture.

Prefer:

> “Document processing occurs on-device.”

over:

> “Your data is completely private.”

The first can be technically verified.

The second may imply guarantees beyond the architecture.

---

# 51. Performance Claims

Avoid:

> “Lightning fast.”

Prefer:

> “Median processing time: X seconds under the tested conditions.”

Performance claims should include context.

# 52. AI Model Claims

# 59. Demo Copy

A demo should tell visitors:

```text
What am I about to do?
What will happen?
Where will processing occur?
What should I look at?
```

---

# 60. Error Copy

Errors should be factual.

Bad:

> “Oops! Our AI got confused 🤖”

Better:

> **Processing failed**

> The demonstration did not complete under the current environment.

> [Try Again]

If a fallback exists:

> [View Recorded Result]

# 61. Copy Density

Recommended hierarchy:

### Hero

1–3 sentences.

### Project overview

2–4 short paragraphs.

### Technical section

Short explanations + structured data.

### Experiment

Compact but rigorous.

### Field note

Short and observational.

Avoid making every page a long-form essay.

---

MAKATA.ai should repeatedly invite the visitor to ask:

```text

How does it work?

What failed?

What changed?

What are you investigating now?
```

These questions should become the site's editorial backbone.

# 63. Content Creation Workflow

When a new project is added:

```text
1. Define the problem
2. Define the system
3. Capture evidence
4. Document architecture
5. Record constraints
6. Record failures
7. Record lessons
8. Link experiments
9. Publish project
10. Add to relevant indexes
```

Do not begin by writing a polished marketing story.

# 65. Editorial Rule

A new page should answer:

> **What does this page add that another page does not?**

If the answer is unclear, do not publish the page.

---

# 66. Evidence Rule

Every significant technical claim should have one of:

```text
Measurement
Observation
Architecture evidence
Demonstration
Experiment
Source/reference
```

If none exists, classify the claim as a hypothesis or remove it.

---

# 67. Contradiction Rule

When two experiments produce conflicting results:

**Do not automatically reconcile them.**

Instead:

```text
Experiment A
Result X

Experiment B
Result Y

Possible explanations
...

Unresolved
...
```

This is more scientifically and technically honest than forcing a single narrative.

---

# 68. Historical Rule

Do not silently rewrite old thinking.

If an old hypothesis was wrong:

```text
Original hypothesis
↓
Evidence
↓
Revision
```

Preserve the evolution.

This makes MAKATA.ai a record of engineering thought rather than merely a collection of finished outputs.

# 71. Final Copywriting Principle

MAKATA.ai should not say:

> **“Look how impressive I am.”**

It should create the experience:

> **“Look at this system.”**

Then:

> **“Here is how it works.”**

Then:

> **“Here is what happened when I tested it.”**

Then:

> **“Here is where the assumptions broke.”**

Then:

> **“Here is what I changed.”**

And finally:

> **“Here is what I'm investigating next.”**

The credibility should emerge from the sequence.
